# Example 3: Weak Academic Paragraph → Strong Academic Paragraph

**Transformation**: Vague, unsourced student writing → Evidence-dense, properly argued scholarship
**Passes used**: Pass 1 (Diagnostic) → Pass 3 (Surgery) → Pass 4 (Voice)
**Voice**: academic rigorous
**Genre**: academic
**Illustrative rubric score**: 38 → 88 (a teaching judgment, not a measured result)

---

## Before (Score: 38/100)

Social media has had a significant impact on mental health, particularly among young people. Many studies have shown that excessive use of platforms like Instagram and TikTok can lead to increased rates of anxiety and depression. Furthermore, the constant comparison to others on these platforms can negatively affect self-esteem. It is important for parents and educators to be aware of these risks and take steps to mitigate them. Additionally, social media companies should take more responsibility for the well-being of their users, especially minors.

### Violations Found
- "Many studies have shown" — which studies? Name them.
- "Significant impact" — how significant? Quantify.
- "Furthermore" + "Additionally" — AI transition stacking
- "It is important" — filler hedge
- Every sentence is about the same length and weight, so no beat stands out (descriptive)
- Zero named sources, zero data points
- No counterargument
- Conclusion is a recommendation with no evidence basis
- Reads identically to 10,000 other AI-generated paragraphs on this topic

---

## Source cards (synthetic — what the student supplied)

The studies below are **invented for this page**. They stand for sources a student would actually have read; they are not real publications and the figures are not findings of any real research. A real rewrite uses only sources the author supplies and marks any gap `[needs source]`.

- **S1** — *Longitudinal survey of adolescents* (n = 500,000, invented). Teens at three or more hours daily had a higher risk of depressive symptoms than those at one hour or less; the rise is sharpest around the two-hour mark.
- **S2** — *Meta-analysis* of 27 studies (invented). Argues the mechanism is displaced sleep, activity and face-to-face time, not screens themselves.
- **S3** — *Population analysis* (n = 350,000, invented). Finds technology use explains a very small share of well-being variance; criticizes self-reported screen time and cross-sectional designs.
- **S4** — *Reply to S3* (invented). Argues population-level effects can mask concentrated harm in vulnerable subgroups.

## After (illustrative score: 88/100)

The first source card, a large longitudinal survey of adolescents (S1), found that teens spending three or more hours a day on social media were at higher risk of depressive symptoms than those spending one hour or less — but the relationship was not linear: the sharpest rise came near the two-hour mark, which suggests a threshold rather than a simple dose-response curve. A later meta-analysis (S2) argues the mechanism is not screen time itself but what it displaces — sleep, physical activity and face-to-face contact — each of which may protect independently. The size of the S1 risk increase is [needs source: the card gives a direction and a threshold, not a percentage].

The counterevidence deserves honest weight. A separate population analysis (S3) found that technology use explains only a very small share of the variance in well-being, and it criticizes the field's reliance on self-reported screen time and on cross-sectional designs that cannot establish cause. The reply (S4) is that a small average effect can hide concentrated harm in vulnerable subgroups. Whether that reply holds depends on subgroup data none of these cards supplies.

### What Changed
- Four source cards in place of "many studies" — the student's own reading, not a brand-name citation the rewriter invented
- Direction, threshold and design limits stated; the one number the cards do not give is marked `[needs source]`
- Counterargument presented at its strongest (S3), with the reply (S4) and a plain note about what would be needed to settle it
- Concession in the author's voice: "The counterevidence deserves honest weight"
- Sentence lengths follow the thought: long where a finding and its limit travel together, short where a verdict lands (descriptive, not a target)
- The v8 version cited named researchers, years, journals and statistics that no source in the repository anchored; those are removed
