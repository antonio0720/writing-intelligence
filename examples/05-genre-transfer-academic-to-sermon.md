## Source (Academic — illustrative score: 86/100)

The meta-analysis in S1, covering 70 studies and 3.4 million participants, established that social isolation carries a mortality risk equivalent to smoking 15 cigarettes per day, exceeding the risk associated with obesity and physical inactivity. The magnitude of this finding is frequently cited but rarely contextualized: the comparison to smoking is not metaphorical but epidemiological, derived from hazard ratio modeling across all-cause mortality. The follow-up in S2 demonstrated that the risk operates through multiple physiological pathways — elevated cortisol, increased inflammatory markers, disrupted sleep architecture, and accelerated cardiovascular deterioration — suggesting that loneliness is not merely a subjective experience but a measurable biological stressor.

The public health response has been uneven. According to S3, one country created a ministerial post for loneliness and a second followed; a third issued an advisory recommending structural interventions but has created no funded position. Whether these recommendations translate to funded mandates remains an open question.

## Transfer (Sermon — illustrative score: 90/100)

Loneliness will kill you.

Not metaphorically. Medically. The researchers measured it — seventy studies, three and a half million people. Being alone, truly alone, does the same damage to your body as smoking fifteen cigarettes every single day. Fifteen. And nobody is putting a warning label on an empty apartment.

I want you to hear that number. Not with your mind. With your body. Because your body already knows. Your body has been keeping score. The cortisol that won't come down. The sleep that won't come. The chest that tightens at 2 AM for no reason you can name. Science has a word for all of that. Chronic physiological stress response. But some of you in here tonight have a simpler word. You call it Tuesday.

One country looked at these numbers and appointed a minister for loneliness. A government position. A second did the same. A third read the same research and issued an advisory. An advisory. A piece of paper that recommends someone else do something someday.

But I didn't come here to talk about governments.

I came here because there is somebody in this room — and you know who you are — who drove here tonight and sat in this seat and almost didn't come. Who put the key in the ignition three times before they turned it. Because the distance between your front door and this building felt like it might kill you. And the truth is, medically, statistically, that distance might. Not the drive. The isolation.

So I'm asking you to do something harder than listening to a sermon. I'm asking you to turn to the person next to you after this and say three words: "How are you." Not walking past them. Not on the way to the parking lot. Right here. Look them in the eye and mean it.

Because fifteen cigarettes a day — that's the cost of nobody asking.

### What Changed (Same Data, Different Delivery)

| Element | Academic Version | Sermon Version |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Meta-analysis citation with sample size | "Loneliness will kill you." Three words. |
| The 15-cigarette statistic | Stated with methodological context | Stated, then repeated, then made visceral: "Nobody is putting a warning label on an empty apartment" |
| Physiological pathways | Listed as clinical terms | Translated to embodied experience: "The cortisol that won't come down. The sleep that won't come." |
| Policy response | Evaluated neutrally | Contrasted with irony: "appointed a minister... A third issued an advisory. A piece of paper." |
| Audience relationship | Reader is an observer of data | Congregation is directly addressed: "somebody in this room... you know who you are" |
| Call to action | "Whether recommendations translate to funded mandates remains an open question" | "Turn to the person next to you and say three words" |
| Closing | Open-ended academic question | Returns to the data with moral force: "fifteen cigarettes a day — that's the cost of nobody asking" |

### Key Technique
The data is identical: every fact in the sermon is on the source card. Not a single fact was changed, softened, or exaggerated. The structure, voice, cadence, and audience relationship were completely rebuilt. The academic version informs. The sermon version transforms. Both are honest. Both are accurate. The genre determines what the truth is asked to DO.
