## v9: Shape Follows the Thought

The shapes above are descriptions of what working paragraphs tend to do, not templates to impose. Choose a shape because the thought has that structure (a claim with its support, a real conflict, a sequence of events), and let the paragraph be as long as the thought. Three paragraphs of the same length, with the same opening move and the same closing turn, are usually a sign that the thought was fitted to a form. Repair that by returning to the proposition each paragraph carries (the thought inventory in `references/v9/EDITORIAL_PROTOCOL.md`, gate E03). If two paragraphs carry the same proposition, merge them; if one carries none, cut it.

- **Evidence Paragraph.** Claim, Source, Data and Interpretation need a source that exists. If the source has not been supplied, mark the paragraph `needs_source`; do not fill the data slot with a plausible figure.
- **Contrast Paragraph.** Position A and B must be positions someone holds. A straw alternative added to complete the shape earns nothing (protocol gate E07). Where the "not X, but Y" form recurs, test whether X and Y are different facts; if they are, keep the form once.
- **Narrative and Scene Paragraphs.** In non-fiction the moment and the detail belong to the author's account or a cited source. A composite or reconstructed scene is labeled.
- **Compression and Question Paragraphs.** Keep a short paragraph short because the thought is short, not to vary lengths. A deliberately uneven rhythm is legitimate when it follows from the meaning; adding unevenness to change how a text reads to a scanner is not an editing reason here.

The Paragraph Physics Audit Checklist's cadence checks are reading aids. Where one conflicts with the author's meaning, the meaning wins.
