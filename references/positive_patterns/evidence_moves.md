# Evidence Moves

## Principle

Evidence doesn't speak for itself. Every piece of evidence needs three things:
introduction (what it is and where it comes from), presentation (the evidence
itself), and interpretation (what it means in context). Missing any one of
these three is a failure.

---

## Source First (v9)

In v9 the evidence comes before the move. The author or a named source supplies the number, case, quotation or event; the move only decides where it sits in the paragraph. If no source supplies it, the right output is a note saying what is missing (`needs_source`), not a plausible-looking substitute. A move is also not a way to raise or lower any detector reading; choose it because the reader needs that kind of support at that point.

**Every example below is synthetic.** Names, institutions, figures and quotations in the examples illustrate the *shape* of each move. They are not findings, were not checked, and must not be copied into a real document. Where an example resembles a real person or event, treat the resemblance as accidental or as unverified. When you use a move for real, the figure and attribution must come from a source you have read, with the citation kept.

---

## Move Types

### The Data Anchor
Introduce a specific number that grounds the argument.

Pattern: Claim → "In [year], [source] found/reported [specific data]" → Interpretation

Example (synthetic):
"Remote workers report higher productivity — but the gains aren't distributed
equally. A hypothetical 2023 survey of 1,600 employees at a fictional firm,
Hartwell Logistics, found that fully remote workers completed 10% fewer
collaborative tasks than hybrid workers, while independent work showed no
difference."

### The Case Citation
Name a specific instance that illustrates the pattern.

Pattern: General claim → "Consider [specific named case]:" → Details → What it shows

Example (synthetic; the real case that inspired the pattern must be sourced before reuse):
"Supply chain concentration creates fragile systems. When a single supplier's
plant in a fictional town, Alder Falls, shut for six weeks after a fire, a
carmaker that bought the part from nowhere else stopped three assembly lines.
One factory. One fire. Three lines."

### The Expert Voice
Bring in a named authority whose expertise is relevant.

Pattern: Context → "[Named expert], [credential], [verb: argues/observes/warns]
that [specific claim]" → Your interpretation

Example (synthetic; the expert is invented):
"The optimism around the timeline may be premature. Dr. Ines Varga, a
fictional plasma physicist with 25 years of reactor research, argues that the
published results routinely exclude the energy cost of operating the magnets
and heating systems."

### The Contrast Pair
Present two pieces of evidence that create tension with each other.

Pattern: Evidence A → Evidence B → "The gap between A and B suggests/reveals..."

Example (synthetic numbers; no real classification is being reported):
"Institution A classified 47 fictional countries as 'low income' in a given
year. Institution B held active programs in 42 of them. The near-total overlap
raises a question worth asking: has the lending changed the classification of
any country on the list? The paragraph then has to answer from the sources,
not from the pattern."

### The Temporal Sequence
Show change over time to establish a trend or trajectory.

Pattern: "[Metric] was [X] in [Year A]. By [Year B], it was [Y]." →
Interpretation of the rate, direction, or inflection point.

Example (synthetic series):
"In a fictional annual poll, trust in the local paper stood at 72% in Year 1
and 16% in Year 47. The decline wasn't linear: it held above 50% until Year 28,
then fell. The inflection point coincides with a change the writer can name and
source, and the paragraph says what the source shows and what it does not."

### The Concessive Evidence
Present evidence that works AGAINST your argument, then show why your
argument holds anyway.

Pattern: "The strongest evidence against [my position] is [specific data/case]."
→ Why it doesn't invalidate the argument → What it does reveal

Example (synthetic figures; a real version cites the report and page):
"The strongest evidence against the proposal is a fictional agency's projection
that it would eliminate 1.3 million jobs. That number deserves respect. But the
same report projected 17 million workers with direct income gains. The question
isn't whether job losses occur; it is whether the net effect outweighs losses
concentrated in specific sectors, and the paragraph argues that from the
report's own tables."

## Evidence Integration Rules

1. Never drop a quote or statistic without introduction and interpretation
2. Vary signal phrases — don't use "According to" more than twice per 1000 words
3. Paraphrases must change BOTH vocabulary and structure from the original
4. Short quotes (under 15 words) integrate into the sentence flow
5. Long quotes (15+ words) get block formatting with preceding introduction and following analysis
6. Every piece of evidence must connect explicitly to the paragraph's claim
7. If evidence is ambiguous, say so — forced certainty is dishonest
8. Primary sources outrank secondary sources
9. Recent evidence outranks older evidence unless the older evidence is foundational
10. A single strong piece of evidence beats three weak ones

## When the Evidence Is Missing

A move needs a source in hand. When the source is absent, do one of: ask the author for it, mark the claim `needs_source`, rewrite the claim to what is known, or cut it. Do not borrow a real-sounding example from this file. Where a competing source disagrees, keep both visible (see `references/diagnostics/source_conflict_detection.md`). A passage whose support is only a plan or a model (a prototype, a forecast) says so in the sentence: planned, prototyped, measured and released are separate states.
