## 1. Hard Bans

These phrases announce, pad or perform instead of saying something. Revise them on sight in the author's own prose, because each one delays the claim. They are not proof of who wrote a passage: people have always written them. The exceptions are quoted speech, titles, and an author's established idiom (see section 7).

### Setup / Throat-Clearing
- "Here's the thing"
- "Here's the deal"
- "Here's what you need to know"
- "Let's be clear"
- "Let's be honest"
- "Let's break it down"
- "Let's unpack this"
- "Let's dive in"
- "Let's explore"
- "Make no mistake"
- "To be sure"
- "It's worth noting"
- "It goes without saying"
- "It bears mentioning"
- "It's important to understand"
- "It's crucial to recognize"
- "It's no secret that"

### Temporal Cliché
- "In today's world"
- "In today's landscape"
- "In today's economy"
- "In an era of"
- "In a world where"
- "In the ever-evolving"
- "In this day and age"
- "As we navigate"
- "As we stand at the crossroads"

### Pseudo-Depth
- "At its core"
- "At the end of the day"
- "When all is said and done"
- "When the dust settles"
- "The real question is"
- "The question isn't X — it's Y"
- "It's not just X — it's Y"
- "Not only X, but Y"
- "It's less about X and more about Y"

### Manufactured Drama
- "Think about it"
- "Let that sink in"
- "Read that again"
- "Spoiler alert"
- "Plot twist"
- "Here's the kicker"
- "Wait for it"
- "Brace yourself"

### Punctuation as Mic
- "Full stop."
- "Period."
- "Mic drop."
- "End of story."
- "That's it. That's the post."

### Buzzword Compounds
- "Game-changer"
- "Paradigm shift"
- "Double-edged sword"
- "Silver bullet"
- "North star"
- "Low-hanging fruit"
- "Move the needle"
- "Deep dive"
- "Unpack"
- "Lean in"
- "Level up"
- "Circle back"

### False Intimacy
- "I see you"
- "I hear you"
- "You're not alone in this"
- "And that's okay"
- "Give yourself grace"
- "Your feelings are valid"
- "It's okay to not be okay"
(Allowed ONLY in therapeutic, pastoral, or explicitly personal contexts with a real referent)

## 2. Soft Bans

Remove unless they demonstrably earn their place. The test: does removing it change the meaning or force of the sentence? If not, it goes.

### Adverbs
- Remove ornamental adverbs: "truly," "really," "very," "incredibly," "extremely," "absolutely," "fundamentally," "essentially," "literally" (figurative), "undeniably," "unquestionably," "remarkably"
- Keep precision adverbs ONLY when removal changes factual meaning: "She arrived late" (keep), "She arrived incredibly" (cut)
- Keep manner adverbs when they specify how: "He spoke quietly" is fine. "He spoke really quietly" loses "really."

### Em Dashes
- Default: remove. Replace with period, comma, colon, or restructure.
- Exception: retain when the em dash creates a timing effect that no other punctuation achieves. One per 500 words maximum.

### Semicolons
- Allowed in formal/academic prose and lists
- Banned in casual, conversational, or sales prose
- Never use to connect unrelated clauses

### "However" as Opener
- Default: restructure the contrast into the sentence
- Exception: allowed when the contrast is the paragraph's entire point and "however" opens cleanly

### Parenthetical Asides
- Under 8 words: allowed
- Over 8 words: restructure as a separate sentence or footnote

### "And" / "But" as Sentence Openers
- Maximum: once per 500 words each
- Must serve rhythm, not laziness

## 3. Earned Exceptions

These moves are powerful when intentional and weak when habitual. Allow only with clear purpose.

### Rhetorical Questions
- **Allowed**: When they open genuine tension or redirect the reader's frame
- **Banned**: When they substitute for argument ("Isn't that what we all want?")
- Test: Does the question create uncertainty? Or does it assume its own answer?

### One-Sentence Paragraphs
- **Allowed**: For impact after a dense passage, or to mark a pivot
- **Banned**: More than once per 800 words. Never consecutive.
- Test: Would combining it with the adjacent paragraph lose force?

### Fragment Sentences
- **Allowed**: For rhythmic punch in narrative, speech, or sales
- **Banned**: In academic, legal, or precision prose
- Test: Is the fragment intentional craft or incomplete thought?

### Intentional Repetition
- **Allowed**: Anaphora, epistrophe, or structural repetition for rhetorical force
- **Banned**: Same idea restated in different words (padding)
- Test: Remove the repetition. Did you lose rhythm or just words?

### Manufactured Quotables
- **Banned**: Lines formatted for pull-quote extraction but hollow in context
- **Allowed**: Lines that earn memorability through the quality of the thought
- Test: Is this memorable because of what it says, or how it's punctuated?

## 4. Inflated Verbs

Replace with precise, honest alternatives:

| Inflated | Usually Means |
|---|---|
| revolutionize | change, improve, redesign |
| transform | change, shift, adjust |
| reimagine | redesign, rethink |
| redefine | change the definition of, expand |
| disrupt | compete with, challenge, enter |
| empower | enable, allow, give authority to |
| leverage | use |
| optimize | improve, tune, adjust |
| synergize | combine, coordinate |
| catalyze | start, accelerate |
| democratize | make accessible |
| spearhead | lead |
| champion | support, advocate |
| unlock | enable, reveal |
| amplify | increase, strengthen |
| elevate | improve, raise |

Use the inflated verb ONLY when the scale of the action genuinely warrants it.
"The printing press revolutionized information access" = earned.
"Our new dashboard revolutionizes analytics" = inflated.

## 5. Filler Hedges

Remove unless they reflect genuine epistemic uncertainty:

- "It's important to note that" → just state the note
- "It should be mentioned that" → mention it
- "One could argue that" → make the argument or attribute it
- "There is a sense in which" → specify the sense
- "In many ways" → name the ways or cut it
- "To some extent" → specify the extent or cut it
- "Broadly speaking" → be specific or cut it
- "By and large" → specify or cut
- "For all intents and purposes" → cut
- "At the risk of oversimplifying" → just simplify honestly

## 6. Stock Transitions

These transitions are stock moves that announce a relationship instead of showing it. Replace one when the logic it names is not actually there, or when it is the third in a page:

- "Moreover" (once per 1000 words max)
- "Furthermore" (once per 1000 words max)
- "Additionally" (once per 1500 words max)
- "That said" (once per 1000 words max)
- "With that in mind" (once per 2000 words max)
- "This is where" (once per 1500 words max)
- "This brings us to" (hard ban)
- "Which brings us to" (hard ban)
- "Building on this" (hard ban)
- "Taken together" (once per 2000 words max)

Preferred alternatives: structural contrast, juxtaposition, temporal markers,
causal connectors, or simply starting the new paragraph with its own claim.
The strongest transition is no transition — just a paragraph that earns its
adjacency to the previous one.

## 7. v9 Catalog Cross-Reference, Counterexamples and the Review Ledger

The v9 reference kernel keeps a **334-rule editorial catalog** (321 literal, 13 regex, 11 categories) in `prototypes/wi9/rules/v9_hotphrases.json`, scanned by `prototypes/wi9/wi9_hotphrases.py`. It is experimental: v9 is a proposal on PR #8, not a release. This file's lists are the older hand-written ones; where the two overlap, the catalog carries the question and this file carries the craft advice.

**A match is an editorial question, never a verdict.** It does not say who wrote the passage, does not prove anything about origin, and never triggers an automatic rewrite. Every rule asks the author or editor a question; the answer may be "this is fine as written."

| Section of this file | Catalog category | Question the catalog asks |
|---|---|---|
| Buzzword compounds, inflated verbs, temporal clichés | `marketing_abstraction`, `domainless_solutionism` | Which concrete action or measurable change is claimed? Who does what, under which conditions, with what observable result? |
| Setup / throat-clearing, stock transitions | `staged_explanation` | Does this transition help the reader follow evidence, or only announce the structure? |
| Pseudo-depth, punctuation as mic | `empty_emphasis` | What decision, consequence or observation makes this important? |
| "It's not just X, it's Y", "less about X, more about Y" | `overused_rhetorical_structure` (regex rules) | Do both sides name meaningfully different facts? A pattern match alone never justifies a rewrite. |
| Filler hedges | `qualifier_padding` | Is the uncertainty genuine and scoped, or padding? |
| "Let's be honest", "Here's the thing" | `performed_candor`, `empty_emphasis` | Does the phrase reflect the actual speaker, or perform candor? |
| Manufactured drama ("Think about it", "Let that sink in") | `forced_audience_engagement` | Is direct address useful for this audience and genre? |
| Manufactured quotables, closing flourishes | `generic_closure`, `empty_metaphor` | What earned consequence or open question should end the passage? Does the metaphor clarify a mechanism better than direct language? |

### When not to fix

A catalog hit is left alone, or a source is added instead of a rewrite, in these cases. The examples are **synthetic**, built for this page, not quotations from real documents.

- **Quoted or transcribed speech** (`staged_explanation`, `performed_candor`). A reporter quoting a source who said "Let's be honest, we missed the date" preserves what was said. Changing a quotation to fix a phrase falsifies the record.
- **Domain use** (`marketing_abstraction`). "Move the needle" inside a clinical-trial endpoint description may be a literal label from the protocol; check the protocol before touching it.
- **Genre convention** (`forced_audience_engagement`). A classroom handout that says "Ask yourself which assumption you tested first" is doing instruction; direct address is the genre.
- **Earned emphasis** (`empty_emphasis`). "This matters because the dosage changed from 5 mg to 50 mg" has a stated consequence; the phrase is attached to a fact.
- **Scoped uncertainty** (`qualifier_padding`). "It should be noted that the sample excludes under-18 participants" is a limitation, not padding.
- **Structure with two real sides** (`overused_rhetorical_structure`). "The policy was not just delayed but reversed" is correct when delayed and reversed are different events.
- **Idiom the author uses on purpose** (`generic_closure`). A speaker whose ending line is her signature sign-off keeps it; a voice sample (voiceprints) can show that it recurs across her consented writing.

### Recording a keep/revise decision

Use the review ledger, `prototypes/wi9/wi9_phrase_review.py`, so the decision is bound to the exact text and rule pack rather than remembered:

1. Scan: `python3 prototypes/wi9/wi9_hotphrases.py scan --input draft.txt --format json > report.json`. The report carries the document and rule-pack sha256 digests and each hit's offsets.
2. Write a `wi9.editorial.review/1` decisions file: the same `document_sha256` and `rules_sha256`, and one entry per hit with `rule_id`, `start`, `end`, `excerpt`, a `status`, a `rationale` of at least twelve characters, a `reviewer` and an offset-aware `reviewed_at`.
3. `status` is one of `retain_as_written` (a legitimate use, say why), `revise_for_clarity` (the thought is sound; the wording hides it), `needs_source` (the claim is unsupported; add a source or cut it) or `not_applicable` (the match is outside the rule's meaning).
4. Run `python3 prototypes/wi9/wi9_phrase_review.py --input draft.txt --report report.json --decisions decisions.json`. It returns `REVIEW` or `HOLD` (stale document or rules, unknown span, duplicate, missing rationale). It always reports `release_authorized: false`.

Any edit to the draft changes its digest and makes the old decisions stale; re-scan. The reviewer name is declared, not authenticated, and a reviewed phrase is not proof of origin or of source truth. Editing a phrase to change a scanner's output, rather than to say the thing better, is outside this system's purpose.
