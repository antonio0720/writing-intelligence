## Consolidated requirements 1–180


Each item is an addition or strengthening of an existing canonical primitive. `B` means carried-forward baseline, `N` a new notes-driven requirement. Numbering is stable for implementation and test planning.

### Prose Intelligence — prior recommendations (01–12)

1. **B001 — Metaphor Saturation Governor.** Detect excessive similes, metaphors, and poetic interpretations across passages; recommend selective edits without flattening voice.

2. **B002 — Figurative Language Value Test.** Require major imagery to add perception, emotional consequence, characterization, or thematic meaning rather than paraphrase the previous line.

3. **B003 — Manuscript-Wide Repetition Detection.** Count repetitive expressions and sentence patterns by chapter and across the complete manuscript, reporting locations rather than guessing quality.

4. **B004 — Metaphor Family Registry.** Distinguish deliberately recurrent imagery from overused image families and semantic equivalents.

5. **B005 — Plain Prose Counterweight.** Protect direct physical description, ordinary sentences, and restraint as deliberate parts of literary rhythm.

6. **B006 — Narrative Overexplanation Detector.** Flag passages where exposition names the meaning of an already intelligible dramatic image.

7. **B007 — Emotional Declaration Restraint.** Find repeated statements of emotion that could be carried more effectively by action, dialogue, thought, or consequence.

8. **B008 — Sentence-Rhythm Diversity Audit.** Evaluate fragment chains, parallel constructions, paragraph length, and narration cadence over sustained passages.

9. **B009 — Authorial Intrusion Detector.** Catch narration that announces a chapter's or book's intended meaning without explicit metafictional justification.

10. **B010 — Sensory Progression Engine.** Track recurring sensations and require evolution in cause, meaning, stakes, or consequence.

11. **B011 — Descriptive Compression Operator.** Identify redundant description while safeguarding atmosphere, orientation, voice, and character.

12. **B012 — Prose Preservation Rule.** Forbid automatic deletion by word-frequency threshold; edits require contextual review and author approval.


### Thriller & Suspense Intelligence — prior recommendations (13–24)

13. **B013 — Escalation Novelty Law.** New danger beats must alter exposure, constraints, knowledge, relationships, or options; heightened adjectives alone do not qualify.

14. **B014 — Tension Plateau Detector.** Find adjacent scenes repeating the same pressure without a dramatic state change.

15. **B015 — Threat Consequence Requirement.** Demand a concrete demonstrated consequence of a major threat before mystery is prolonged too far.

16. **B016 — Threat Specificity Test.** Separate disquiet from recognizable danger and mark where a reader obtains actionable understanding.

17. **B017 — Suspense Information-Asymmetry Engine.** Track knowledge differences among reader, protagonist, allies, and antagonists at scene and act boundaries.

18. **B018 — Earned Mystery Doctrine.** Preserve uncertainty over answers while orienting the reader to the operative question.

19. **B019 — Mystery Debt Ledger.** Track raised questions, clues, partial answers, reveals, and deliberately deferred mysteries across a series.

20. **B020 — Failure-Driven Escalation.** Favor complications causally generated by characters' attempts rather than unrelated arbitrary interruptions.

21. **B021 — Antagonist Competence Standard.** Keep adversarial choices strategically credible and bounded by established abilities and resources.

22. **B022 — Escalation Variety Requirement.** Vary physical, social, relational, institutional, informational, psychological, and moral pressure.

23. **B023 — Tension Recovery Discipline.** Preserve genuine relief, humor, intimacy, and normal life rather than one sustained maximum intensity.

24. **B024 — Chapter-Ending Integrity.** Reject arbitrary cliffhangers that depend on withheld information characters already know.


### Character & Emotional Intelligence — prior recommendations (25–34)

25. **B025 — Human Before Function Law.** Treat narrative and professional functions as secondary to desire, relationships, memories, contradictions, and agency.

26. **B026 — Personal Desire Conflict Engine.** Ensure major characters can want things that conflict with duty, ideology, loyalty, or one another.

27. **B027 — Costly Rule Violation Test.** Make rule-breaking psychologically credible, causally legible, and consequence-bearing.

28. **B028 — Distinct Character Decision Profiles.** Differentiate people through decisions under stress as well as dialogue mannerisms.

29. **B029 — Relationship Evolution Ledger.** Track shifts in trust, intimacy, status, obligation, resentment, and betrayal over time.

30. **B030 — Emotional Memory Continuity.** Carry psychological consequences into later scenes without implausible automatic reset.

31. **B031 — Human Stakes Translation.** Bind large-system threats to intimate, recognizable consequences in bodies, relationships, memory, and homes.

32. **B032 — Character Agency Verification.** Find turning points where characters merely observe a mechanism; require consequential agency or explicit helplessness.

33. **B033 — Competence Without Emotional Uniformity.** Permit expertise and personality, weakness, bias, humor, grief, and irrationality to coexist.

34. **B034 — Emotional Payoff Audit.** Require earned dramatization of losses, reunions, betrayals, sacrifices, and wins.


### Worldbuilding & Speculative Fiction — prior recommendations (35–44)

35. **B035 — World Rules Constitution.** Model fictional capacities, exclusions, conditions, costs, and exceptions; distinguish established fact from character belief.

36. **B036 — Power Boundary Enforcement.** Prevent convenient ability expansion unsupported by canon or explicit storyworld exception.

37. **B037 — Cost Conservation Doctrine.** Track debts and consequences of extraordinary interventions without narrative erasure.

38. **B038 — Victory Versus Closure Distinction.** Separate objective completion, legitimacy, moral correctness, physical safety, and enduring resolution.

39. **B039 — Consent and Authority Conflict Model.** Track who can act, who claims permission, who actually holds authority, and who bears costs.

40. **B040 — Timeline Perspective Registry.** Distinguish event, document, observation, narration, and record-revision times.

41. **B041 — Canon Contradiction Classification.** Distinguish deliberate paradox, mistaken character belief, unreliable narrator, unresolved mystery, and actual continuity defect.

42. **B042 — Speculative Mechanism Demonstration.** Show fictional rules through actions, limitations, and costs rather than abstract lectures alone.

43. **B043 — Scientific Claim Boundary.** Label real science, contested interpretation, fictional extrapolation, and invented mechanisms differently.

44. **B044 — Worldbuilding Restraint.** Reject worldbuilding additions that provide no causal, emotional, aesthetic, or narrative benefit.


### Novel Architecture & Series — prior recommendations (45–52)

45. **B045 — Novel-Length Escalation Graph.** Measure structural change in stakes, vulnerability, information, and irreversibility across all chapters.

46. **B046 — Act-to-Act Transformation Test.** Ensure major phases change the protagonist's choices, identity, relationships, or understanding.

47. **B047 — Third-Act Acceleration Audit.** Detect aftermath scenes that restate victory consequences without producing new changes.

48. **B048 — Opening Promise Verification.** Compare opening emotional/genre promises and mysteries with their delivery later in the novel.

49. **B049 — Payoff Quality Engine.** Audit setup-to-payoff causal legitimacy and emotional yield, not only matching references.

50. **B050 — Ending Without Explanation Law.** Flag explicit declarations that a book is ending or that its final line is significant.

51. **B051 — Standalone Satisfaction / Series Continuation Test.** Test local arc closure separately from unresolved long-series hooks.

52. **B052 — Emotional Center Protection.** Check that spectacular systems do not displace the human conflict at the center of the novel.


### Editorial & Publication — prior recommendations (53–60)

53. **B053 — Front Matter Separation.** Disentangle necessary story orientation from author manifestos, marketing, and optional essays.

54. **B054 — Document Placement Validator.** Find glossaries, guides, and explanatory materials inserted at distracting or impossible reading positions.

55. **B055 — Mathematical and Symbol Rendering Verification.** Detect broken LaTeX, escapes, Unicode, equations, and layout in produced editions.

56. **B056 — Publishing Metadata Completeness.** Report unfinished ISBN, edition, imprint, attribution, and production placeholders as applicable.

57. **B057 — Timeline and Cross-Book Reference Proofing.** Validate date/event references and serialized continuity without suppressing deliberate nonlinear histories.

58. **B058 — Typesetting and Pagination Audit.** Check formatting, spacing, widows/orphans, and print/ebook usability in addition to word counts.

59. **B059 — Independent Reader Evaluation.** Collect observations from human readers on suspense, confusion, emotional impact, and satisfaction.

60. **B060 — Genre-Specific Publication Gate.** Never infer publication readiness solely from grammatical, structural, or canon test passes.


### Want/Need & Ending Semantics — new from notes (61–70)

61. **N061 — Conscious External Want Contract.** Record the concrete outcome the character knowingly seeks, its owner, earliest statement, and whether its pursuit drives external plot. [Notes pp.1–2]

62. **N062 — Latent Internal Need Contract.** Record the proposed inner development, why it matters, when/if the character recognizes it, and who is interpreting it. [Notes pp.1,5]

63. **N063 — Want/Need Version History.** A want may be replaced, reprioritized, attained, abandoned, or become a need; preserve prior states instead of overwriting history. [Notes pp.2,4,6]

64. **N064 — Outcome Quadrant Calculator.** Provide sweet, semi-sweet, bittersweet, and bitter as a transparent 2×2 **view** of want/need outcomes, not an exhaustive theory of narrative endings. [Notes pp.1,3,5,7]

65. **N065 — Nonbinary Outcome Vocabulary.** Support fulfilled, partially fulfilled, relinquished, sacrificed, thwarted, unknown, disputed, and not-applicable rather than forcing every ending to yes/no.

66. **N066 — Want Versus Need Collision Model.** Track scenes in which attaining the visible goal compromises inner need, or satisfying the need requires giving up the goal. [Notes pp.4,6–9]

67. **N067 — Multiple Simultaneous Wants.** Support competing goals at different levels, including immediate scene objectives, act-level targets, and a changed central goal. [Notes pp.2,4,6]

68. **N068 — Need Interpretation Provenance.** Treat 'this character needs X' as author-declared craft interpretation, not an objectively discoverable truth about a person.

69. **N069 — Multi-Protagonist Ending Matrix.** Allow different characters to occupy different outcome quadrants; avoid requiring one universal ending label.

70. **N070 — Ending Valence Independence.** Represent hope, dread, irony, moral cost, justice, closure, and audience feeling separately from want/need status.


### Ghost, Lie, Truth & Choice — new from notes (71–80)

71. **N071 — Character Ghost With Causal Evidence.** Represent formative wounds, betrayals, losses, and experiences as possible causes of present defenses without assuming all behavior arises from trauma. [Notes pp.5–7]

72. **N072 — Misbelief or Lie Representation.** State the distorted rule a character treats as true and how it predicts their choices. [Notes pp.7–9]

73. **N073 — Truth Counterposition Registry.** Record competing values or perspectives that challenge the lie; do not declare one interpretation morally absolute by automation.

74. **N074 — Valued-Good Inventory.** Identify something meaningful the character already possesses but undervalues, especially when a negative arc endangers it. [Notes pp.7–9]

75. **N075 — Characteristic Moment Plant.** Plant a telling early behavior that demonstrates temperament, capability, contradiction, or vulnerability. [Notes pp.3–4]

76. **N076 — Foreshadowed Potential Test.** A corruption, redemption, or major reversal should be causally legible from early evidence without becoming predictable. [Notes pp.3–4]

77. **N077 — Crisis of Belief Engine.** Track the moment a character's action collides with the belief that guided earlier actions.

78. **N078 — Revelation Versus Transformation Distinction.** A character recognizing a truth is not evidence of lasting change; require later behavioral consequence.

79. **N079 — Choice-as-Proof Rule.** Evaluate whether the climactic decision demonstrates adoption, rejection, or ambiguity of a belief instead of relying only on dialogue.

80. **N080 — Truth-Rejection Escalation.** In negative arcs, track each credible opportunity to change and the character's chosen refusal, with accumulating harm. [Notes pp.7–9]


### Character Arc Families & Dynamics — new from notes (81–90)

81. **N081 — Positive Change Arc Specification.** Model flaw/defense, catalytic pressure, competing truth, costly actions, new behavior, and its consequences. [Notes pp.5–7]

82. **N082 — Flat Arc Specification.** Allow the protagonist's core conviction to remain stable while their influence transforms other characters or the environment. [Notes p.2]

83. **N083 — Corruption Arc Specification.** Distinguish gradual embrace of morally destructive choices from sudden arbitrary villainy. [Notes pp.3–5]

84. **N084 — Fall Arc Specification.** Track the repeated failure to confront a central misconception and the good that is progressively sacrificed. [Notes pp.7–9]

85. **N085 — Disillusionment and Open Arc Support.** Represent arcs ending in wiser pessimism, unchanged belief, unresolved identity, or deliberate anti-transformation.

86. **N086 — Arc-Type Versus Ending-Type Independence.** Never infer positive/flat/negative trajectory solely from sweet/semi-sweet/bitter outcome, or vice versa.

87. **N087 — Impact Character Graph.** Track the character whose value, choices, example, or confrontation pressures the protagonist to reconsider. [Notes p.6]

88. **N088 — Truth-Bearer and Lie-Bearer Roles.** Represent people who embody competing models of a meaningful life without flattening them into moral instruction devices. [Notes pp.8–9]

89. **N089 — Transformation of Secondary Characters.** For flat protagonists, explicitly test whether meaningful change occurs in supporting people or institutions. [Notes p.2]

90. **N090 — Character Agency Across Arc.** Every major arc transition must identify what the character chose, resisted, or suffered; distinguish forced change from chosen change.


### Arc Causality, Midpoints & Reversals — new from notes (91–100)

91. **N091 — Page-One Ending Seed.** Trace the eventual outcome to early desire, belief, flaw, resource, promise, or conflict, rather than retrofitting a finale. [Notes p.2]

92. **N092 — Catalyst and First Threshold.** Record the event that makes the established want harder, reveals a new want, or forces commitment.

93. **N093 — Midpoint Revaluation.** Support mid-story reinterpretation of the want, the lie, relationships, identity, or the nature of victory without hard-coding a 50% timestamp. [Notes pp.7–8]

94. **N094 — Want Replacement Causal Chain.** Verify that a new central goal grows from events and does not erase the significance of the original goal. [Notes pp.2,4,6]

95. **N095 — Incremental Moral Thresholds.** Show small choices making larger choices increasingly plausible, particularly in corruption arcs. [Notes pp.3–5]

96. **N096 — Genuine Alternatives Before Sacrifice.** A final sacrifice matters more if the easier or desired alternative was genuinely attainable at a cost.

97. **N097 — Minor Defeat and Reversal Chain.** Make positive endings earned through real resistance, loss, recovery, and altered tactics rather than effortless reward. [Notes pp.2–3]

98. **N098 — Character's Shadow Cost Accounting.** Track whose needs, safety, relationships, or possibilities are harmed by pursuit of the protagonist's desire. [Notes pp.3–5,7–9]

99. **N099 — Turning-Point Counterfactual.** Ask whether removing a stated turning point changes later decisions; if not, the claimed turning point may be decorative.

100. **N100 — Final State Versus Starting State Diff.** Produce an evidence-linked comparison of the character's beliefs, relationships, conduct, wants, and losses at opening and closing scenes. [Notes pp.7–9]


### Finale, Catharsis & Reader Experience — new from notes (101–110)

101. **N101 — Earned Sweet Ending Test.** Require meaningful adversity, loss risk, and causally earned reconciliation before allowing both goal and need to be satisfied. [Notes pp.2–3]

102. **N102 — Semi-Sweet Renunciation Test.** Verify that loss or sacrifice of a want materially expresses fulfilled inner need, rather than random deprivation. [Notes pp.5–7]

103. **N103 — Bittersweet External Victory Test.** Distinguish visible external success from interior emptiness, broken relationships, or moral defeat. [Notes pp.7–9]

104. **N104 — Bitter Ending Integrity.** Make loss of both want and need causally connected to decisions or constraints, not imposed for artificial bleakness. [Notes pp.3–5]

105. **N105 — Tragic Satisfaction Paradox.** Allow readers to feel triumph, sympathy, dread, or admiration even when the protagonist's moral and personal trajectory is devastating. [Notes pp.3–5]

106. **N106 — Catharsis Through Consequence.** Ground emotional release in irreversible action, changed relationship, recognition, or loss rather than declarative speeches. [Notes pp.1,5–7]

107. **N107 — Ending Irony Composer.** Support outcomes where attaining the stated instrument destroys the underlying value, such as connection versus status. [Notes pp.8–9]

108. **N108 — Ending Echo With Difference.** Return to an opening object, choice, gesture, relationship, or claim whose meaning has changed by the finale.

109. **N109 — Last-Scene Character Proof.** Use observed conduct and changed circumstances to demonstrate final arc state without narrator explanation. [Notes pp.7–9]

110. **N110 — Reader Response Versus Author Intent.** Measure readers' perceived satisfaction/ambiguity separately from the writer's intended arc or ending quadrant.


### V8 Compiler, Testing & Governance — new requirements (111–120)

111. **N111 — Canonical Arc Nodes, Not a Second Database.** Extend existing `fictional_canon` and storyworld graph semantics with `narrative.want`, `narrative.need`, `narrative.belief`, `narrative.ghost`, `narrative.arc_turn`, and `narrative.ending` as typed nodes or compatible extension fields.

112. **N112 — State-Time-Aware Arc Transitions.** Attach `valid_from`, `valid_until`, `known_at`, provenance, character, and causing scene/event to changes in desire and belief; preserve decision-time meaning.

113. **N113 — Two-Layer Tests: Deterministic and Interpretive.** Test IDs, continuity, dependencies, transitions, and matrix projections deterministically; send psychological credibility and catharsis to qualified review.

114. **N114 — Fiction-Safe Proof Vocabulary.** State `consistent_with_canon`, `author_asserted`, `reviewer_inferred`, `reader_reported`, or `unverified`; never call aesthetic excellence machine-proven.

115. **N115 — Adversarial Fiction Benchmark Set.** Test flat arcs, positive and negative arcs, partial outcomes, changed wants, multi-protagonist conflicts, anti-formula stories, and deceptively polished but emotionally hollow endings.

116. **N116 — Negative Controls Against Template Tyranny.** The system must not automatically fail intentional nonlinear arcs, tragedy, unchanged protagonists, ambiguous endings, or endings without a single revelation.

117. **N117 — Source Provenance & Fair-Use Discipline.** Preserve origins and rights status for the notes' film examples and named theorist; create new explanatory wording and original test fiction rather than reproducing protected scripts.

118. **N118 — Writer Override With Rationale.** Permit author-authorized deviations from recommended craft patterns without silently altering canonical facts; preserve reasons, scope, and version history.

119. **N119 — Independent Reader Calibration.** Compare automated findings with blinded human readers, including false-positive/false-negative findings and variance by genre and audience.

120. **N120 — Receipt-Governed v8 Release.** Freeze a versioned program/manifest, enumerate actual executable checks, run negative and regression tests, preserve v4–v7 behavior, and forbid `shipped` without inspectable command/result/readback receipt.


## A. Opening-Scene Intelligence — C121–C131

121. **C121 — Opening Strategy Repertoire.** Encode a selectable library of opening approaches: character entrance, antagonist entrance, relationship demonstration, catalyst, in-medias-res, flashback, flashforward, dream, puzzle, quiet power play, parallel action, iconic moment, concealed clue, and others from the notes. **Acceptance:** author can choose, combine, or decline techniques; compiler reports purpose and downstream obligations, never rejects an original opening for not matching a template. **Source:** pp.2–6.

122. **C122 — Hook-Relevance Audit.** Differentiate a spectacular opening that honestly establishes the work's primary conflict from unrelated spectacle optimized for momentary attention. **Acceptance:** map the opening event to later causal, emotional, or thematic consequences; flag disconnected prologues without demanding an explosion or immediate violence. **Source:** pp.2–6.

123. **C123 — Opening Baseline and Disturbance Selector.** Support violent immediate entry *and* calm-before-chaos, quiet dread, comic disarmament, or emotionally charged normalcy. **Acceptance:** baseline, disruption, and changed reader expectation are identifiable when relevant; the system accepts quiet, atmospheric literary openings. **Source:** pp.3–4.

124. **C124 — Multi-Layer Genre Promise.** Represent what the first scene promises separately about genre, tone, protagonist, danger, mystery, and narrative perspective. This is an operational extension of B048, not a second promise ledger. **Acceptance:** later scenes can be traced to each promise; intentional genre flips carry enough early signals to be fair. **Source:** pp.2–4.

125. **C125 — Early Orientation Without Exposition.** Track minimum scene coordinates—where, whose perspective, present action, local intention, what is knowable—while preserving mystery. **Acceptance:** test-reader confusion about basic geography/chronology is distinguished from productive curiosity about a puzzle. **Source:** pp.3–6.

126. **C126 — Nonlinear Opening Disclosure Contract.** For flashforward, reverse chronology, first-person retrospective, dream, and unreliable-voice starts, specify what time/perspective the audience initially believes and which later scene reinterprets it. **Acceptance:** temporal or viewpoint concealment is author-declared, not generated as accidental inconsistency. **Source:** pp.2, 5–6.

127. **C127 — Character Entrance Through Choice.** Generate or analyze entrances where a meaningful decision, refusal, risk, competence, or revealing flaw establishes identity more effectively than biography or appearance. **Acceptance:** the character's action predicts or complicates a later choice; appearance-only introductions are flagged for review, not universally prohibited. **Source:** pp.2–4.

128. **C128 — Power Entrance Without Violence.** Make antagonist or protagonist authority legible through changes they cause in other people's behavior, setting, resources, silence, and attention. **Acceptance:** readers can identify a credible asymmetry before any exposition about rank or power. **Source:** pp.4, 6.

129. **C129 — Opening Tone Inversion Design.** Construct deliberate contrast between an opening's apparent tone and the larger story's tone without mis-selling the work. **Acceptance:** record the first incompatible cue, decisive reveal, and intended reader reinterpretation; require an early way to recognize the contrast retrospectively. **Source:** pp.3–4.

130. **C130 — Hidden-in-Plain-Sight Clue Contract.** Design an innocuous object, action, sound, or presence whose later significance is fair and reconstructable, with exposure and salience recorded. **Acceptance:** readers given the reveal can locate the original clue without retroactive insertion; a twist depending on an unseen fact is flagged. **Source:** pp.5–6.

131. **C131 — Fair Red-Herring and Unreliable-Opening Protocol.** Distinguish a character's false belief, a deliberately misleading image, an unreliable speaker, and an authorial falsehood. **Acceptance:** a later reveal changes interpretation without requiring unsupported sensory impossibility or breaking declared viewpoint rules. **Source:** pp.2, 6.

## B. Fight & Action Intelligence — C132–C145

132. **C132 — Set/Rise/Twist/Tie Action Model.** Offer the *Set–Rise–Twist–Tie* framework noted in the source as an optional way to structure a confrontation; keep its claimed cultural provenance unverified unless separately sourced. **Acceptance:** a fight can use an alternative architecture and still pass if its decisions and consequences work. **Source:** pp.7–8.

133. **C133 — Credible Personal Stakes in Combat.** Separate magnitude of hypothetical loss from the character's believable attachment, exposure, and probability of loss. **Acceptance:** the fight's stakes can be stated in one consequential, character-specific proposition; routine 'save the world' language alone is not treated as emotional power. **Source:** pp.7–9.

134. **C134 — Combat Agency and Objective Map.** For each combatant track the immediate objective, escape objective, restrictions, and how objectives change under pressure. **Acceptance:** major moves can be tied to an intelligible tactical or emotional purpose; choreography that exists only for display is reported. **Source:** pp.9–12, 19–20.

135. **C135 — Fight-Space Geometry and Access Graph.** Record exits, obstacles, cover, elevation, available weapons, sight lines, distances, and changing access during action. **Acceptance:** no actor moves through an inaccessible route or exploits an object whose existence and position were not established. **Source:** pp.7, 9–12.

136. **C136 — Action Viewpoint and Perception Limits.** Simulate which movements, threats, sounds, and injuries a viewpoint character can actually observe under speed, darkness, obstruction, noise, and cognitive load. **Acceptance:** the narration does not silently grant omniscient tactical vision to a limited POV. **Source:** pp.10–16.

137. **C137 — Capability and Fatigue Continuity.** Carry established training, reach, strength, impairment, ammunition, exhaustion, and injury through exchanges and subsequent scenes. **Acceptance:** repeated actions become harder or change meaning when the physical state changes; no unearned recovery or unlimited endurance. **Source:** pp.9–16.

138. **C138 — Objective-Driven Combat Reversals.** Make shifts in advantage result from attempts to obtain, keep, protect, escape, or sacrifice something—not interchangeable blows. **Acceptance:** each reversal changes what at least one combatant can realistically achieve next. **Source:** pp.7–13.

139. **C139 — Repeated-Move Adaptation Test.** If a signature maneuver recurs, track whether the opponent has learned, the environment changed, the timing differs, or the repetition is a deliberate motif. **Acceptance:** an identical move cannot produce identical surprise forever without a causal explanation. **Source:** p.9.

140. **C140 — Tactical Breather with New Information.** Use pauses, circling, repositioning, and silence to reveal competence, geography, fear, injury, or a changed plan. **Acceptance:** the break changes the audience's combat forecast; do not insert pauses solely to match a numeric formula. **Source:** pp.7–8.

141. **C141 — Earned Environmental Twist.** Permit a previously established object, skill, vulnerability, rule, or change in terrain to invert the fight. **Acceptance:** the twist has a visible precondition and does not introduce a convenient new tool without setup. **Source:** pp.8, 12–13.

142. **C142 — Combat Detail Compression by Medium.** Distinguish prose fiction from screenplay action lines. Render selected decisive exchanges at high resolution while compressing repetitive movements; avoid demanding complete blow-by-blow action. **Acceptance:** physical causality remains legible after compression and novelistic interiority is not stripped to imitate scripts. **Source:** pp.9, 13–16.

143. **C143 — Dialogue Under Physical Stress.** Make fight speech serve motivation, distraction, bargaining, provocation, surrender, humor, or an earned callback, subject to realistic breath, pain, distance, and danger. **Acceptance:** remove a line and test whether the fight's intention or relationship changes; do not require silence in every realistic fight. **Source:** pp.19–20.

144. **C144 — Action Soundscape and Rhythmic Restraint.** Use silence, impact, interruption, and selective sound words to orient the reader, not to saturate pages with capitalized effects or ellipses. **Acceptance:** rhythm improves in read-aloud comparison without an automatic onomatopoeia quota. **Source:** pp.13–19.

145. **C145 — Fight Resolution as Character and Plot Turn.** Separate winning the physical exchange from achieving the scene goal and from proving character change. **Acceptance:** the result alters at least one of capacity, relationship, information, moral commitment, or subsequent plot; action for its own sake is flagged. **Source:** pp.8–13.

## C. Villain, Antihero & Opposition Intelligence — C146–C158

146. **C146 — Antagonist Pressure Network.** Model antagonism as multiple agents, institutions, social norms, environments, or internal contradictions rather than requiring one villain in every story. **Acceptance:** causal responsibility for opposition can be assigned without inventing a personalized evildoer. **Source:** pp.20–28.

147. **C147 — Mirror-Villain Divergence Map.** Track traits, experiences, convictions, and methods shared by protagonist and antagonist, then document the consequential choices where they diverge. **Acceptance:** similarity alters at least one decision or moral dilemma instead of existing only as an explanatory parallel. **Source:** p.21.

148. **C148 — Antagonist as Moral Stress Test.** Design antagonist actions that credibly test the protagonist's declared boundaries and identity. **Acceptance:** the attempted test has feasible mechanics, more than one meaningful option, and consequences not resolved by author fiat. **Source:** pp.21–22.

149. **C149 — Looming-Threat Presence Budget.** Support antagonists who remain physically absent while their actions shape characters' options and expectations. **Acceptance:** threat remains causally active via evidence and behavior; absence alone is not treated as mystery. **Source:** pp.22–23.

150. **C150 — Aftermath-as-Adversary Evidence.** Establish villain competence and severity through specific observable consequences, not only fearful testimony or ominous labels. **Acceptance:** each indirect appearance teaches the reader something new about method, limits, or stakes. **Source:** pp.22–23.

151. **C151 — Secondhand Testimony Reliability Graph.** Separate what witnesses saw, heard, inferred, exaggerated, or deliberately concealed about a threat. **Acceptance:** contradictory testimony can coexist without arbitrary omniscient adjudication, and a later reveal is grounded in knowable evidence. **Source:** pp.22–23.

152. **C152 — Motive Reveal Exposure Ladder.** Plan staged revelations of an antagonist's motive, from observable aim through rationalization to underlying desire, without automatically treating an ambiguous motive as unfinished writing. **Acceptance:** later motive revelations reinterpret earlier action without contradicting known opportunity or resources. **Source:** pp.22–23, 28–30.

153. **C153 — Heroism/Morality/POV Multiaxis Model.** Distinguish protagonist status, antagonistic function, sympathetic understanding, ethical conduct, and desired outcome for antiheroes, antivillains, villain protagonists, and conventional villains. **Acceptance:** the model does not classify every self-interested lead as evil or equate reader sympathy with moral endorsement. **Source:** pp.23–28.

154. **C154 — Harm and Sympathy Coexistence.** Preserve a villain's credible attachments, vulnerabilities, virtues, or injustice suffered while accounting for actual harm. **Acceptance:** sympathetic backstory cannot erase victim consequences or force readers to agree with the villain. **Source:** pp.20, 23–26.

155. **C155 — Mentor-Mask Antagonism.** Support villains who first function as guide, ally, patron, or rescuer and exploit trust to advance another purpose. **Acceptance:** the relationship has useful genuine actions and planted contradictions; the reveal alters past-scene interpretation, not merely a label. **Source:** pp.28–30.

156. **C156 — Greater-Good Rationalization Ledger.** Track the antagonist's stated objective, internal standard of success, sacrifices imposed on others, and the beneficiaries who allegedly justify those costs. **Acceptance:** critique and consequence remain available even if the antagonist's plan works technically. **Source:** pp.28–30.

157. **C157 — Private Psychological Utility Behind Public Mission.** Differentiate a mastermind's announced social or political project from intimate needs for recognition, control, validation, belonging, or revenge. **Acceptance:** this divergence predicts behavior at meaningful moments and can be contradicted by actions. **Source:** pp.28–30.

158. **C158 — Villain Archetype Composition Without Type Lock.** Make beast, bully, machine, mastermind, fanatic, henchperson, mirror, anti-villain and related models composable *descriptions*, not exclusive personality types. **Acceptance:** a hybrid antagonist is representable and their decisions arise from the established character, not a stereotype. **Source:** pp.21–28.

## D. Long-Form Suspense & Dialogue Intelligence — C159–C167

159. **C159 — Scene Pledge/Contract Graph.** At an opening or early dialogue exchange, allow a question, offer, risk, withheld explanation, or promise to obligate later dramatic delivery. Extend B019's mystery ledger with scene-specific intention, due point, and payout mode. **Acceptance:** neither trivial bait nor an entirely unrelated answer counts as payment. **Source:** pp.32–33.

160. **C160 — Suspenseful Conversation as Action.** Treat dialogue turns as bids to extract information, test a lie, change power, control time, obtain consent, or conceal a plan. **Acceptance:** a long conversation has shifting positions and an altered end state even without overt physical action. **Source:** pp.32–33.

161. **C161 — Reader-Knows-Danger-First Mode.** Provide controlled dramatic irony in which the reader knows a specific danger before the threatened characters do. This specializes B017's information-asymmetry model for suspense. **Acceptance:** readers can state the approaching consequence while characters have credible reasons not to act yet. **Source:** pp.32–33.

162. **C162 — Suspense Certainty Ramp.** Track whether successive details raise or lower the plausibility of a feared outcome, rather than escalating solely through louder prose. **Acceptance:** a before/after reader forecast identifies what new fact changed the inferred odds. **Source:** pp.32–33.

163. **C163 — Conversational Subtext Intent Contract.** Represent what a speaker says, privately wants, believes the listener knows, and tries to induce. **Acceptance:** surface politeness and hidden threat yield intelligible tactics and a causal audience payoff. **Source:** pp.19–20, 32–33.

164. **C164 — Politeness as Constraint and Threat.** Model scenes where etiquette, dependence, hospitality, professionalism, or social roles make direct refusal dangerous. **Acceptance:** the social cost of breaking the performance is shown; formal civility alone does not count as menace. **Source:** pp.20–23, 32–33.

165. **C165 — Suspense Clock Semantics.** Separate an explicit countdown from implicit clocks (arrival, exposure, loss of trust, a decision deadline, diminishing options) with traceable expiry conditions. **Acceptance:** the time pressure changes character choices; invented countdowns with no causal force are rejected. **Source:** pp.4–5, 32–33.

166. **C166 — Meaningful Silence and Delay.** Distinguish waiting that permits discovery, grants power, raises vulnerability, or causes a cost from delay that merely pads a chapter. **Acceptance:** shortening the pause would change suspense, information, or a choice. **Source:** pp.32–33.

167. **C167 — Reader Forecast and Emotional Participation.** Make a testable prediction about what attentive readers fear, hope, or suspect at selected beats, then compare with real reader reports. **Acceptance:** the system reports reader disagreement and uncertainty instead of manufacturing a single validated reaction. **Source:** pp.32–33.

## E. Sequence Goals, Mixed Victories & Plot Causality — C168–C177

168. **C168 — Hierarchical Goal/Sequence Graph.** Model scene goal → sequence goal → act goal → novel goal, including goal ownership, time bounds, dependencies, and conflicts. **Acceptance:** the analyst can show why each sequence exists and how local results affect the higher objective. **Source:** pp.43–46, 49–51.

169. **C169 — Local Outcome Vector for Every Sequence.** Track objective achieved, resource cost, information gained, relationships harmed, villain benefit, and future options rather than one binary success flag. **Acceptance:** a sequence can be locally successful and globally disastrous without label contradiction. **Source:** pp.45–51.

170. **C170 — Perceived-Now Versus Revealed-Later Result.** Record a character's and reader's judgment at the time of a supposed victory separately from later evidence that it served the opponent. **Acceptance:** later revelations supersede interpretation without rewriting what the characters could know earlier. **Source:** pp.47–51.

171. **C171 — Interlocking Opponent Goal Simulation.** Compare protagonist and antagonist intentions at each sequence and evaluate how either side can exploit the other's action. **Acceptance:** at least one nontrivial alternate opponent response is examined before claiming an intricate trap robust. **Source:** pp.47–51.

172. **C172 — Causal Goal Substitution.** When one opponent's action changes the main goal—from dismantling an institution to neutralizing a specific threat, for example—preserve the causal transition and abandoned obligations. **Acceptance:** the new goal doesn't materialize merely because a new act has begun. **Source:** pp.44–51.

173. **C173 — Twist Dependency Audit.** Every major plot reversal must list necessary earlier events, knowledge, access, resources, timing, and chance conditions. **Acceptance:** removing one required condition either changes the outcome or exposes an alternative route; unsupported guarantees are flagged. **Source:** pp.47–49.

174. **C174 — Bounded Antagonist Foresight.** Disallow mastermind plans that depend on perfect predictions of independent human choices unless the mechanism for control or prediction has been established. **Acceptance:** test the plan against plausible deviations and identify which contingencies genuinely existed. **Source:** pp.47–49.

175. **C175 — Coincidence and Logistical Burden Audit.** Track how people obtained tools, traveled, concealed operations, arranged complex events, and survived unexpected obstacles. **Acceptance:** plot-important coincidences or invisible logistics are explicitly reviewed; not every off-page routine requires dramatization. **Source:** pp.47–49.

176. **C176 — Complexity and Comprehension Budget.** Distinguish productive complexity from a plot that outruns the reader's ability to understand causes and stakes. **Acceptance:** sample readers can reconstruct the major cause-and-effect chain at appropriate checkpoints without having to consult a wiki. **Source:** pp.47–49.

177. **C177 — Reversal Cost and Aftermath Persistence.** When a win masks a setback, maintain both outcomes in the event graph and carry consequences forward. **Acceptance:** later chapters recognize the sacrificed person, compromised institution, depleted capability, or antagonist advantage; a subsequent triumph cannot erase it without causal repair. **Source:** pp.47–51.

## F. Research, Representation & Medium Fidelity — C178–C180

178. **C178 — Responsible Sensory and Cultural Specificity.** Provide reference-assisted descriptions of sound, physical appearance, regional folklore, and setting while reviewing objectification, reductive portrayal, and misuse of living cultural traditions. **Acceptance:** culturally specific source claims are attributed and reviewed; no arbitrary skin-tone metaphors or copied stock descriptors are required. **Source:** pp.1–2, 31–32.

179. **C179 — Domain Verisimilitude With Freshness Checks.** For military maneuvers, policing, institutions, geography, equipment, physics, and similar factual backgrounds, ground concrete operational details in appropriate sources and note time dependence. **Acceptance:** a historical list of FBI investigation programs is not promoted to a current federal claim merely because it appears in a personal research notebook. **Source:** pp.1, 31–32.

180. **C180 — Cross-Medium Narrative Equivalence.** Preserve the dramatic purpose of a scene when adapting a novel to screenplay, audio drama, comic, game, or screen-based narrative, but recompute permissible techniques, pacing, and audience access to information. **Acceptance:** screenplay assumptions such as page-to-minute ratios or capitalized impact sounds do not become mandatory rules for literary prose; the same core decision and consequence remain traceable. **Source:** pp.10–20; existing cinematic narration and transmedia packages.
